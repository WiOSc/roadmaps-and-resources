# Creative Coding: A Practical Guide to Making Things With Code

> You do not need to be an experienced programmer to make something interesting with code.
>
> Make a little thing. Make it move. Make it react to your mouse. Make it ugly. Break it. Accidentally make it beautiful. Then make something stranger.

Creative coding uses programming as a medium for art, music, games, interactive websites, procedural worlds, visual experiments, installations, typography, simulations, and stories.

This guide is a **choose-your-own path**, not a syllabus you must complete. Follow the thing that makes you go, *“Wait, someone made that with code?”* You can come back for the rest later.

---

## Start with what interests you

You might be here because you like:

- ✨ Pretty things, generative art, patterns, posters, or animations.
- 🎮 Games, simulations, virtual worlds, particles, or weird physics.
- 🎧 Music, sound design, live visuals, or visuals that react to a beat.
- 🌐 Websites that feel like art projects instead of another boring landing page.
- 🧊 Blender, 3D scenes, procedural cities, impossible architecture, or alien landscapes.
- 🪄 Shaders, digital glitches, fluid effects, and GPU wizardry.
- 🤖 AI, computer vision, webcam interactions, or projects that respond to people.
- 🧰 Physical computing: sensors, LEDs, projectors, Arduino, ESP32, and installations.
- 📚 Generative writing, interactive fiction, data storytelling, or digital poetry.
- 🧪 Experiments that are not obviously useful—and are more interesting because of it.

**All of these count.** Creative coding is not a competition to build the most commercially useful thing. Sometimes the project is a tool. Sometimes it is a toy. Sometimes it is just a rectangle that has a very emotional day.

---

## The beginner rulebook (there are barely any rules)

1. **Start with the result you want to see, not a language you think you should learn.**
2. Copy a tiny example, run it, and change one thing. Then change five more things.
3. You are allowed to learn by remixing. Credit the person whose work you build on.
4. Do not wait until you “know enough.” The project is how you learn enough.
5. Save versions. `final_final_really_final_7.js` is funny until it is your only backup.
6. Share unfinished experiments. A sketchbook is allowed to contain sketches.
7. Take breaks when a bug turns your brain into microwave static.

> **The creative-coding loop:** See something → wonder how it works → make a tiny version → break it → tweak it → show someone → repeat.

## 1. Generative art: let rules make the picture

**The idea:** You write a set of rules; the computer creates an image or family of images. It can be geometric, organic, chaotic, minimal, colourful, quiet, or completely cursed.

**Try making:**
- A thousand circles that avoid your cursor.
- A poster where every refresh creates a different composition.
- A digital garden where flowers grow according to mathematical rules.
- A map of imaginary constellations.
- A collection of album covers generated from a single seed.
- A “mood machine” that creates a palette and pattern from a word you type.
- A printable series of 100 unique art prints.

**Start with**
- [p5.js](https://p5js.org/) — friendly JavaScript creative coding.
- [The Coding Train](https://thecodingtrain.com/) — beginner videos and coding challenges by Daniel Shiffman.
- [OpenProcessing](https://openprocessing.org/) — explore, run, remix, and publish sketches.
- [Processing](https://processing.org/) — the original sketchbook-style creative coding environment.
- [py5](https://py5coding.org/) — Processing-inspired creative coding in Python.

**First project — The 100 Posters Challenge:** Draw a poster using only circles, lines, and three colours. Add randomness. Press a key to generate a new version. Save your five favourites.

**Level up:** use Perlin noise, vector fields, typography, SVG export, or a seeded random generator so you can recreate a design you love.

## 2. Motion, particles, physics, and tiny artificial life

**The idea:** Give objects simple rules—move toward the mouse, avoid each other, follow a field, attract, repel, flock, grow, or disappear. Complicated behaviour can emerge from a few small rules.

**Try making:**
- A flock of birds that moves like one creature.
- Fireflies that gather around your cursor.
- A swarm that writes your name and then dissolves.
- A black hole that bends particles around it.
- A flock of manta rays flying through a canyon.
- A little ecosystem where organisms compete for food.
- A crowd simulation or traffic system.
- A screen full of agents that draw paths and slowly erase them.

**Explore**
- [The Nature of Code](https://natureofcode.com/) — vectors, forces, autonomous agents, cellular automata, and more.
- [Coding Train challenges](https://thecodingtrain.com/challenges) — a giant source of visual experiments and simulations.
- [p5.js examples](https://p5js.org/examples/) — start small, then remix.
- [NumPy](https://numpy.org/) and [Matplotlib](https://matplotlib.org/) — useful for Python-based math and visualisation.

**First project — Cursor Constellation:** Particles drift around the screen, connect when close, and flee when the cursor approaches. Add a toggle for “calm” and “chaos.”

**Level up:** flocking (Boids), reaction-diffusion, L-systems, cellular automata, strange attractors, fluid-like fields, or evolutionary systems.

## 4. 3D art, procedural modelling, and Blender sorcery

**The idea:** Instead of manually building every detail, write rules that generate objects, architecture, plants, rocks, rooms, landscapes, or entire collections of scenes.

**Try making:**
- A procedural city with buildings that follow different rules.
- A thousand alien plants.
- A tiny room that feels like a memory.
- A low-poly planet with rings and moons.
- A machine that assembles a new sculpture every time.
- An impossible building or a library with infinite corridors.
- A music track translated into 3D cover art.
- A canyon full of clouds you can fly through.

**Explore**
- [Blender](https://www.blender.org/) — free 3D creation suite.
- [Blender Python API](https://docs.blender.org/api/current/) — build scenes and automate Blender with Python.
- [Geometry Nodes manual](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/modeling/geometry_nodes/index.html) — node-based procedural modelling.

**First project — The Weird Object Factory:** Create one object from a few parameters: number of rings, height, twist, material, and seed. Generate 20 variations and render them as a gallery.

**Level up:** Geometry Nodes, particle scattering, procedural materials, terrain erosion, animation, Python batch rendering, and interactive web viewers.

## 5. Shaders, digital goo, and GPU magic

**The idea:** A shader is a small program that runs on the GPU and can change how pixels or surfaces look. It is behind many of those impossible glowing, liquid, dreamy, and glitchy visuals.

**Try making:**
- A liquid-metal orb.
- An animated aurora.
- A fake portal to another dimension.
- A water surface with ripples.
- A pixelated CRT screen effect.
- A procedural cloud or nebula.
- A landscape made entirely from maths.
- A shader that turns your webcam into a moving mosaic.

**Explore**
- [The Book of Shaders](https://thebookofshaders.com/) — approachable shader lessons.
- [Shadertoy](https://www.shadertoy.com/) — browse and experiment with live shader code.
- [Inigo Quilez](https://iquilezles.org/articles/) — procedural graphics, distance functions, and raymarching.
- [Three.js](https://threejs.org/docs/) — shaders and GPU visuals in the browser.
- [WebGPU](https://webgpu.io/) — modern GPU computing and graphics for the web.

**First project — The Forbidden Orb:** Make a circle that pulses, distorts, and changes colour over time. Start with a simple gradient; do not begin by trying to render an entire universe.

**Level up:** GLSL, signed distance functions (SDFs), raymarching, domain warping, feedback effects, post-processing, and GPU simulations.

## 9. Physical computing: code that touches the world

**The idea:** Sensors, cameras, LEDs, motors, microcontrollers, and physical materials become part of your creative work.

**Try making:**
- A lamp that changes colour with the room's sound.
- A plant-inspired sculpture with light that slowly grows.
- A desk object that displays the weather.
- A sensor-driven generative art installation.
- An LED matrix that shows a live particle simulation.
- A physical button that changes a website's world.
- A tiny weather station that makes a new artwork from its readings.
- A sound-reactive object made from cardboard, LEDs, and a microcontroller.

**Explore**
- [Arduino getting started](https://www.arduino.cc/en/Guide)

**First project — A Physical Mood Ring:** Read a light or sound sensor and use the value to change a colour palette on screen or on an LED. Start with one sensor and one clear behaviour.

**Tiny safety note:** check voltage and current requirements, use suitable power supplies, and do not connect unknown voltages directly to a microcontroller pin.

## 10. AI, computer vision, and responsive art

**The idea:** Machine learning can become an input for creative work. Your project might respond to pose, hand movement, objects, text, sound, or a model's interpretation of an image.

**Try making:**
- Particles that follow your hands through a webcam.
- A digital mirror that transforms your pose into a creature.
- A drawing tool controlled by body movement.
- A soundscape that reacts to objects in view.
- A text-to-pattern generator.
- A visual diary built from your own notes.
- An installation that responds to the distance between people.
- An interactive creature with a simple behaviour system.

**Explore**
- [ml5.js](https://ml5js.org/) — approachable ML experiments in JavaScript.
- [The Coding Train](https://thecodingtrain.com/) — machine-learning and creative coding tutorials.
- [TensorFlow.js](https://www.tensorflow.org/js) — machine learning in JavaScript.
- [p5.js](https://p5js.org/) — create the visual side of the experience.

**First project — Hand-Controlled Galaxy:** Track a hand or use a simple mouse-based stand-in first. Let its position attract particles and its openness change their colour or speed.

**Be thoughtful:** if you use a camera or microphone, explain what it does, ask for consent, and prefer local processing when practical. Do not collect or upload people's data without a clear reason and permission.

### How to explore a project without just copying it

1. Find one sketch or artwork you genuinely like.
2. Ask: what are the moving parts? Shapes? Noise? Particles? Camera? Sound? Data?
3. Run it or study the source if it is available.
4. Change one parameter and observe what happens.
5. Replace one element with your own idea.
6. Give credit and link the original when you publish a remix.
7. Write down what you learned—even if the result is a disaster.

**Your goal isn't to make someone else's project again. It's to discover the part that makes you want to make your own.**

# A gentle path from “hello world” to “what is this thing?”

You don't need to learn every tool. Here is one possible route:

### Step 1 — Make something appear
Use the [p5.js Web Editor](https://editor.p5js.org/). Draw a circle, move it, change its colour, and make it react to the mouse.

### Step 2 — Add a rule
Make 50 circles. Give each a slightly different size or speed. Add randomness, then try noise.

### Step 3 — Give it a personality
Let the viewer influence it. Add a click interaction, a slider, a keyboard shortcut, or a sound.

### Step 4 — Make a finished little piece
Add a title, choose a palette, refine the motion, and make a screenshot or short recording. Small and finished beats huge and abandoned.

### Step 5 — Share it
Publish a sketch on [OpenProcessing](https://openprocessing.org/) or put it on GitHub. Explain what it does and what you learned.

### Step 6 — Follow your curiosity
Want 3D? Try Blender or Three.js. Want sound? Try Sonic Pi or Tone.js. Want GPU magic? Try The Book of Shaders. Want a physical object? Try Arduino.

### Step 7 — Build a personal collection
Keep your experiments in one place. Your portfolio doesn't need to look professional on day one. It needs to show that you are exploring.

# A tiny creative-coder glossary

- **Generative art:** artwork made using a system of rules, code, randomness, or input data.
- **Procedural generation:** creating content—such as terrain, buildings, or patterns—through algorithms.
- **Noise:** smooth, correlated variation often used for natural-looking movement, textures, and landscapes.
- **Particle system:** many small objects following simple rules to create a larger effect.
- **Shader:** a program that runs on the GPU to control pixels, materials, or rendering effects.
- **Live coding:** writing and changing code as part of a live performance.
- **Interaction:** a way for a person, sensor, or other input to influence what the program does.
- **Remix:** taking an existing work as a starting point and transforming it, with appropriate credit and respect for its licence.
- **MVP:** the smallest version that lets you test the core idea. It does not need to be embarrassing forever; just small enough to finish.
- **Happy accident:** a bug that looks so good you decide it was a feature.

# Before you go

You are not behind because you haven't learned GLSL. You are not less creative because you started with a tutorial. You do not need a powerful GPU, an art degree, expensive software, or a ten-year plan to begin.

You need a small idea and the willingness to try it.

Make a circle that behaves like it's lonely. Make a website for a fictional civilisation. Turn rain data into music. Build a cloud that follows your cursor. Make a game with one button and an unreasonable amount of drama.

Then show someone.

**The point isn't to become the kind of person who makes creative things someday. The point is to make one today.**

*Start small, follow your curiosity, and make something of your own.*
